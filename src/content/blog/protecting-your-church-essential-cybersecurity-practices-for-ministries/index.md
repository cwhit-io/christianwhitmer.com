You lock the front doors and count the offering with care—but what about the digital doors to your church? From email scams to lost laptops, ministries face the same cyber threats as businesses, often with fewer resources. The good news: a handful of practical steps can dramatically reduce your risk and protect the people you serve. Here’s how to safeguard your church’s data and systems without becoming a full-time IT professional.

## Why Cybersecurity Matters for Ministries

Churches steward sensitive information every day—donor records, prayer requests, counseling notes, children’s check-in details, and volunteer background checks. A breach can harm trust, interrupt ministry operations, and even put people at risk. Cybersecurity isn’t about fear; it’s about faithful stewardship. By building simple, repeatable habits, your staff and volunteers can keep ministry moving and data safe.

## 1) Lock Down Accounts with Strong Access Controls

Think of accounts (email, church management software, finance systems) as keys to your ministry. Protect them like you’d protect a master key ring.

- Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) everywhere: Email, giving platforms, payroll, cloud storage, social media, and your church management system (ChMS). MFA stops most account takeovers even if a password leaks.
- Use a password manager: Provide staff and key volunteers a trusted password manager to generate and store unique, complex passwords. Discourage sharing passwords by text, email, or paper.
- Right-size permissions: Assign role-based access (e.g., finance team can see donations; kids ministry can see family check-in info). Avoid “everyone is an admin.” Review permissions quarterly.
- Offboard swiftly: When staff or volunteers transition, disable their accounts the same day. Change passwords on shared accounts and remove access to email, calendars, storage, and ChMS.
- Separate admin from everyday use: Create a dedicated admin account for IT tasks and a regular account for daily work. Only use admin credentials when necessary.

These steps make compromised accounts much less likely—and limit the blast radius if one is breached.

## 2) Keep Devices and Networks Healthy

Healthy devices and networks are like well-maintained buses: fewer breakdowns, safer passengers.

- Update everything: Turn on automatic updates for Windows/macOS, browsers, mobile apps, routers, and printers. Schedule a monthly “patch check” for sanctuary PCs and kiosks.
- Install reputable endpoint protection: Use a trusted antivirus or endpoint security tool on staff computers. For larger churches, consider endpoint detection and response (EDR) managed by your IT partner.
- Encrypt and lock devices: Enable full-disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac). Require screen locks and PINs on phones and tablets. Make “lost device” reporting part of policy.
- Inventory equipment: Keep a simple list of staff laptops, church-owned tablets, check-in stations, and their locations. Note serial numbers and who’s responsible.
- Segment Wi-Fi: Create separate networks for staff, guests, and devices (printers, smart TVs, streaming gear). Change default router passwords, use strong WPA2/WPA3 encryption, and disable unnecessary features like UPnP.
- Secure IoT and AV devices: Change default passwords on cameras, soundboard controllers, and streaming encoders. Place them on a separate VLAN or isolated network if possible.

When the network is tidy and devices are current, attackers have fewer cracks to slip through.

## 3) Train Your Team to Spot Scams and Protect Email

Email is the front door for most attacks. A few simple habits can prevent costly mistakes.

- Slow is safe: Teach everyone to pause before clicking links or opening attachments. Verify unexpected requests—especially anything involving money or credentials—by calling the sender on a known number.
- Run short, regular training: A 15-minute quarterly refresher beats a long annual lecture. Share real examples of church-targeted scams (gift card requests from “the pastor,” urgent vendor payment changes).
- Add technical defenses: Ask your IT partner to set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC for your domain to reduce spoofing. Enable anti-phishing and spam filtering. Provide a “Report Phishing” button in the email client.
- Establish finance controls: Require dual approval for ACH/wire transfers. Confirm vendor bank changes using a secondary channel (not email). Keep an “approved payees” list and train staff who handle funds.
- Guard pastoral confidentiality: Avoid sending sensitive counseling notes or prayer request details via email. Use secure, access-controlled notes in ChMS or a protected pastoral care system. Delete old, unnecessary emails containing personal information.

Email security is equal parts technology and team habits. Reinforce both.

## 4) Back Up Data and Respect Privacy

Data protection is about preserving ministry continuity—and honoring the privacy of your congregation.

- Use the 3-2-1 rule for backups: Maintain at least three copies of your data, on two different media, with one off-site or cloud-based. Include servers, worship PC scenes, and cloud data if your provider supports export or backup.
- Test restores quarterly: A backup you can’t restore isn’t a backup. Practice restoring a file and a whole system image. Document the steps.
- Know where your data lives: Map key systems—ChMS, online giving, accounting, payroll, child check-in, prayer requests, small group rosters, background checks. Identify owners and access levels.
- Minimize collection and retention: Collect only what you need. Set retention policies for sensitive data (e.g., delete outdated background checks, purge old attendance exports). Avoid storing Social Security numbers unless required for payroll—and secure them.
- Use secure storage: Store sensitive documents in access-controlled cloud folders or encrypted devices. Lock paper files in cabinets. Avoid personal email and thumb drives for ministry records.
- Communicate privacy clearly: Add brief privacy statements to forms (online and paper) explaining how data will be used and how people can request updates or removal where appropriate.

Backups and privacy practices protect your people and your mission when technology hiccups or storms hit.

## 5) Prepare a Simple Incident Response and Continuity Plan

Planning ahead reduces panic and downtime if something goes wrong.

- Create a contact sheet: List key contacts—IT support, denominational tech help, ChMS vendor, bank fraud line, cyber insurance, local law enforcement. Store a copy offline.
- Define roles and steps: Who investigates? Who decides to disconnect a device? Who communicates with staff and congregation? Write it down; keep it simple.
- Practice annually: Run a tabletop exercise—simulate a ransomware incident or email breach. Walk through decisions and refine your plan.
- Keep ministry operating: Maintain printed backup rosters for services, a manual check-in process, and offline giving alternatives. Document how to run Sunday services if Planning Center or ProPresenter is down.
- Consider cyber insurance: For many churches, a basic policy can cover incident response, legal guidance, and recovery support. Review coverage with a knowledgeable agent.

An incident plan turns a crisis into a manageable process—and keeps worship and care going.

## A 30-Day Starter Plan

If you’re wondering where to begin, here’s a realistic month-long roadmap:

- Week 1: Turn on MFA for email, ChMS, and finance systems. Install a password manager and set a policy for shared credentials.
- Week 2: Enable automatic updates, encrypt staff laptops, and separate guest/staff Wi-Fi. Change default passwords on printers and AV equipment.
- Week 3: Run a 15-minute phishing awareness session. Implement dual approval for payments and vendor changes. Add a “Report Phishing” button.
- Week 4: Set up 3-2-1 backups and test a restore. Map your key data systems and permissions. Draft a one-page incident plan with contact numbers.

Small, consistent steps beat big, complex projects. Each improvement lowers risk and builds confidence.

## Encouragement for the Journey

Cybersecurity doesn’t have to be intimidating or expensive. It’s a ministry discipline—protecting the flock, the resources entrusted to you, and the integrity of your witness. Start with the basics, involve your team, and celebrate progress. When you lock your digital doors and teach wise habits, you create a safer space for people to worship, serve, and grow.

If you need help, ask a trusted IT volunteer or a church-focused tech partner to walk with you. You’re not alone—and with a few practical practices, your church can be both welcoming and well-protected.
